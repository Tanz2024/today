Most advice about making friends assumes you can just walk up to strangers and start talking. If that were easy for you, you probably wouldn't be reading this. Being shy doesn't mean something's wrong with you socially — it usually means the first move costs you more energy than it costs other people, so the trick is making that first move smaller and repeatable rather than trying to force yourself into being someone you're not.

## Lower the bar for what counts as "making a move"

You don't need a great opening line. You need literally anything that opens a door a crack. "Is this seat taken?" "Do you know if we needed the reading for today?" "I like your bag." None of these are impressive. All of them work, because the goal isn't to be charming — it's to create one small, normal interaction that could turn into a second one.

## Go where the same people show up repeatedly

Friendship usually forms through repeated low-pressure contact, not one big conversation. A club, a class you have twice a week, a part-time job, a gym you go to at the same time — these work better for shy people than parties or one-off events because you don't have to make the whole friendship happen in one conversation. You can be slightly awkward the first time and still see them again next week.

<Callout>
Proximity plus repetition beats charisma. You will make more friends seeing the same ten people every week than meeting a hundred new people once.
</Callout>

## Ask questions instead of performing

If small talk drains you, stop trying to be entertaining and start asking questions instead. People generally like talking about themselves, and a genuine question ("how'd you end up doing this?" "what's your family like?") takes the pressure off you to be interesting and puts the conversation on rails. You just have to listen and follow up.

## Let silence be normal

One thing that trips up shy people specifically: treating every pause as a failure. Comfortable silence is a sign of an actually relaxed conversation, not a sign that you're bad at this. If you feel a pause coming, resist the urge to fill it with something forced — often the other person will fill it themselves, or the silence just passes and nobody remembers it.

## Be the one who follows up

Shy people often wait to be invited rather than doing the inviting, which means a lot of promising conversations quietly die because neither person makes the next move. You don't need to be smooth about it — "we should get food sometime, I'm free most weekends" is enough. Sending that text is uncomfortable for about ten seconds and then it's done.

## Give it more time than feels reasonable

Friendships that last usually take longer to form than people expect — research on adult friendship formation suggests it can take dozens of hours together before someone becomes a real friend rather than an acquaintance. If you're shy, you're not behind because it's taking a while. That's just how long it actually takes, for everyone, shy or not.

## The real skill isn't confidence

It's tolerance for a few awkward seconds. Every friendship you have now started with an interaction that could have gone slightly wrong and didn't ruin anything. The goal isn't to stop being nervous — it's to keep doing the small thing anyway, often enough that the nerves stop being the main character.
