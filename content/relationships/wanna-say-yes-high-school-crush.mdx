There are songs you enjoy, and then there are songs that quietly unlock a version of you that you thought you had packed away for good.

For me, that song is [“Wanna Say Yes” by Matt Schuster](https://open.spotify.com/search/Wanna%20Say%20Yes%20Matt%20Schuster). I heard it at the exact wrong time: late at night, half-listening, when one line suddenly made me think of the person I had liked in high school and never really stopped wondering about.

Not because we had some huge, dramatic love story.

Because we almost did.

## The crush who lived in the almost

His name was the kind of name I could spot from the other end of a crowded hallway.

He sat two rows ahead of me in history. He borrowed my notes even though I knew he had his own. I learned his coffee order without ever asking. We had the kind of conversations that felt ordinary while they were happening and important the second they ended.

There was always a reason not to say anything.

I had an exam.

He was seeing someone, maybe.

We were both tired.

The school year was almost over.

Then graduation arrived, and the almost became a memory.

That is the strange thing about an old crush: sometimes you are not missing the person as much as you are missing the version of yourself who still believed something could happen.

## Why this song brought it all back

“Wanna Say Yes” has that particular kind of romantic pull that makes a small memory feel cinematic.

Suddenly, I could remember the blue classroom door. The sound of sneakers on the corridor floor. The way he looked at me when I made him laugh and then looked away a little too quickly.

The song did not make me believe we had secretly been soulmates.

It reminded me that I had been brave enough to feel something, but not brave enough to be honest about it.

That distinction matters.

Nostalgia is very good at editing the past. It softens awkward silences, adds golden light to boring afternoons and turns a person into a collection of perfect details. A song can make you miss a feeling without proving that you should rebuild your life around the person who gave it to you.

## I searched his name anyway

Of course I did.

I told myself it was harmless. One quick search. One glance at an old profile. One tiny check to see whether he had become exactly the person I imagined or someone completely different.

He looked happy.

Not movie happy. Real happy. A little older, slightly more tired, still making the same crooked smile in photographs.

For a minute, I felt ridiculous for caring.

Then I realised the feeling was not ridiculous. It was just unfinished.

## The message I almost did not send

I wrote:

“This is random, but I heard a song that reminded me of high school and thought of you. Hope you’re doing well.”

I stared at it for so long that my phone dimmed.

Then I sent it.

There was no grand confession. No instant declaration. No perfectly scripted reunion.

He replied the next morning.

“That is random. But in a good way. I was just thinking about that history class the other day.”

It was a small reply. Almost nothing, if you looked at it from the outside.

To me, it felt like somebody had opened a window in a room I had forgotten was locked.

## Sometimes the song is the answer

We did not suddenly become a couple. The song did not turn the past into a promise.

But it gave me something better than a fantasy: a chance to stop wondering.

Maybe that is why certain songs stay with us. They do not always point us back to a person. Sometimes they point us back to the sentence we never finished, the message we never sent or the part of ourselves we were too shy to introduce properly.

If “Wanna Say Yes” has been reminding you of someone, listen to it all the way through before you decide what the feeling means. Maybe you miss them. Maybe you miss being seventeen. Maybe you just like the way the song makes an old memory glow.

And if there is a kind, uncomplicated message you have been putting off, maybe send it.

Not because the past owes you a love story.

Because sometimes you deserve to know what happens after the almost.
