Doomscrolling — endlessly scrolling through bad news, drama, or just an infinite feed with no real destination — feels bad while you're doing it and worse after, and yet the pull to keep going is strong enough that just deciding to stop rarely works on its own. The apps are built by teams specifically optimizing for exactly this behavior, so beating it usually takes changing the setup, not just the intention.

## Understand why it's so hard to stop

Infinite scroll removes the natural stopping points that used to exist in media — the end of a newspaper, the end of a TV episode. Without a clear ending, your brain doesn't get the normal cue that it's time to wrap up, so scrolling continues by default rather than by choice. That's a design decision, not a personal willpower failure.

## Make the app harder to open, not impossible

Deleting an app entirely can feel too extreme and often just leads to reinstalling it within days. A lighter version — moving the app off your home screen, logging out so there's a small delay to log back in, using built-in screen time limits — adds enough friction to interrupt the automatic reach-for-your-phone habit without requiring total willpower.

<GoodToKnow>
The goal isn't zero scrolling. It's breaking the automatic, mindless version of it — the kind where twenty minutes disappear and you can't remember what you actually saw.
</GoodToKnow>

## Replace the trigger, not just the behavior

Doomscrolling often happens in specific moments — right when you wake up, in bed before sleep, any small gap of boredom during the day. Instead of just trying not to scroll in those moments, have something else ready: a different app (music, a podcast), a physical object nearby (a book), or just noticing the boredom without immediately reaching for a phone.

## Curate what you'd see if you do scroll

Since eliminating scrolling completely isn't realistic for most people, controlling what fills the feed matters. Unfollowing or muting accounts that specifically feed the doom part — constant negative news, outrage content, accounts that leave you anxious — changes what a scrolling session actually contains, even if the habit itself doesn't fully go away.

## Keep the phone physically out of the bedroom, if you can

A huge share of doomscrolling happens in bed, at night, when you're tired and your guard is down. Charging your phone outside the bedroom, even in another room just a few steps away, removes the easiest and most common doomscrolling window without requiring any in-the-moment self-control.

## Notice the actual feeling, not just the behavior

Doomscrolling often isn't really about wanting information — it's a stress or boredom response, using the phone as a distraction from something less comfortable underneath. Naming that ("I'm scrolling because I'm anxious about tomorrow, not because I need to know anything right now") sometimes makes it easier to choose a different response.

## Be honest about how it actually makes you feel afterward

A quick, honest check the next time you catch yourself deep in a scroll: how do I feel right now, compared to before I started? Most people notice the answer is worse, not better, and that gap — however small — is real information worth paying attention to instead of scrolling past.

## Progress, not a clean break

Doomscrolling less often, for shorter stretches, with more awareness of when it's happening is a realistic win. Aiming for a permanent, total stop usually backfires into an all-or-nothing mindset where one slip feels like total failure. Small, repeatable changes to the setup around your phone will get you further than a single big resolution ever does.
