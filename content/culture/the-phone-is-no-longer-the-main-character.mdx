The new digital-detox trend is less dramatic than the old one.

There is no ceremonial phone smash. No black-and-white selfie captioned *logging off forever*. No promise that you will become the sort of person who reads a paperback on a train and never checks the group chat.

The more interesting shift is quieter: people are asking whether their phone has become the main character in every part of the day.

It is there during breakfast. It is there during a conversation. It is there when you are supposedly watching one episode. It is there when you open it to check the weather and emerge 28 minutes later with a new opinion about a stranger's kitchen renovation.

## The problem is not being online

Ofcom's [Online Nation 2025 report](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/media-use-and-attitudes/online-habits/online-nation) says adults in the UK now spend an average of four and a half hours online each day. That number is useful, but it does not tell us whether those hours were spent learning a skill, messaging a friend, watching a recipe or falling into a feed designed to keep the next swipe effortless.

The real question is control.

Are you choosing the thing you are doing, or are you just accepting the next thing the phone offers?

That is why the current version of digital minimalism feels more practical than the old, total-break version. It is not about proving that you can live without technology. It is about making technology less demanding when you are trying to study, sleep, work or be with another person.

## The “boring phone” fantasy is really an attention fantasy

People say they want a boring phone, but what they usually want is a boring *default*.

They want the screen to stop suggesting a crisis, a purchase, a stranger's relationship, a new notification and a video that begins with “you need to hear this” before they have even decided what they came to do.

![A person holding a phone while sitting near a window](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1512428559087-560fa5ceab42?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1400&q=80)

You can get closer to that feeling without deleting every app. Put the most distracting ones out of your first screen. Turn off alerts that are not from actual people. Keep one hour of the day deliberately under-programmed.

None of this is a moral test. If a particular app genuinely helps you connect, create or relax, it is not automatically the villain. The point is noticing which tools give something back and which ones quietly charge you in attention.

## A small focus block beats a dramatic reset

The most sustainable intervention is often embarrassingly small: choose one task, set a finite block, and stop when the block ends.

That could be 25 minutes of revision. It could be 25 minutes of clearing email. It could be 25 minutes of doing the one household job you have been avoiding because it has become emotionally larger than it is practically.

Try one focused block with the [BrisaStory Focus Timer](/tools/focus-timer). The point is not to become a productivity machine. It is to give your attention one job at a time, then take the break instead of immediately filling it with another feed.

## The new flex is getting your evening back

The most convincing version of digital wellbeing is not a perfect morning routine posted as content. It is noticing that you finished dinner without checking your phone. It is leaving a message unanswered until tomorrow because it was not urgent. It is being bored for long enough that a real idea arrives.

That is not anti-internet. It is pro-choice.

The phone can still be useful, funny, social and full of things you care about. It just does not need to narrate every quiet moment.
