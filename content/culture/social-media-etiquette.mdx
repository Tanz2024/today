Most social media etiquette isn't written down anywhere, but people absolutely notice when it's ignored — you just rarely find out about it directly, because pointing out a social slight over posting habits feels petty to say out loud. Knowing the unwritten rules saves you from small, avoidable friction with people you actually care about.

## Ask before posting a group photo with everyone tagged

Not everyone wants every photo of themselves posted publicly, and tagging someone in a photo they didn't approve puts it on their profile too, whether they like the photo or not. A quick "posting this, cool?" takes five seconds and avoids putting someone in a position of having to ask you to take it down.

## Don't subtweet or vague-post about a specific person

Posting something clearly about someone without naming them — a vague caption everyone in the friend group can decode — usually reads as more petty than direct, and it puts mutual friends in an uncomfortable position of pretending not to know what's going on. If something needs to be said to someone, say it to them.

<Callout>
If a post is really about one specific person, that's a conversation, not content. Posting instead of talking usually escalates things rather than resolving them.
</Callout>

## Reply to DMs eventually, even if it's just an emoji

Leaving someone on read indefinitely, especially after they've asked a direct question, reads as more dismissive than most people intend it to. You don't have to respond instantly, but a late reply is almost always better than no reply, even if it's brief.

## Don't screenshot private conversations without asking

Sharing a screenshot of a DM or private story reply, even one that seems harmless, removes someone's control over their own words and can spread further than they'd ever want. If sharing something someone said feels worth doing, ask first — it costs nothing and avoids a real breach of trust.

## Big news belongs to the person it happened to

If a friend tells you something exciting privately — an engagement, a new job, good test results — let them post it first if they're going to post it at all. Posting about someone else's news before they've shared it themselves takes away their moment, even with good intentions.

## Comments are public, keep that in mind

A comment that reads as playful teasing to you might read very differently to everyone else scrolling past it, especially without tone of voice to soften it. If a comment would feel weird said out loud in front of a group of strangers, it probably doesn't belong under a public post either.

## When in doubt, default to the version that requires less cleanup later

Most etiquette questions come down to one test: if this goes slightly wrong, how much work is it to undo? Asking before tagging, texting instead of vague-posting, and not screenshotting private messages are all just choosing the version of an action that's easy to walk back if it turns out to bother someone — which, most of the time, avoids the problem altogether.
