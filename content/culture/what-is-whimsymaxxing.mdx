It starts with a coffee cup. Then a phone case. Then a lip balm, a hair clip, a water bottle, a birthday cake and, if the internet is to be believed, at least one entire car. Somewhere along the way, a handful of plastic rhinestones stopped being a craft-drawer leftover and became a whole personality.

The internet has a name for it: whimsymaxxing. And if your feed has recently filled up with gems glued onto things that absolutely did not need gems, you have already met it.

## So, what actually is whimsymaxxing?

At its simplest, whimsymaxxing means taking an ordinary object and making it deliberately, unnecessarily joyful. The most visible version is bedazzling: sticking colourful rhinestones and gems onto everyday things until they sparkle. People also call it gem-maxxing, or just "bedazzling everything."

The examples are gloriously random. Coffee and smoothie cups. Phone cases. Skincare tubs. Celebrity birthday cakes, including the gem-covered one from Kylie Jenner's birthday in August. One car owner went as far as covering a Honda CR-V in rhinestones, which is either art or a very committed cry for help.

![Hands peeling a sheet of colourful rhinestone stickers to decorate a notebook on a desk scattered with craft supplies](https://images.pexels.com/photos/10607182/pexels-photo-10607182.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1600)

But the word stretches further than gems. Whimsymaxxing also covers playful food presentation, loud make-up, novelty accessories and rooms that look lived-in instead of staged. The "-maxxing" bit is internet shorthand for doing something to the extreme. Here, the thing being maximised is fun.

It is not a movement with a manifesto. Plenty of Gen Z people have never glued a gem to anything and never will. It is a mood that keeps popping up in different forms, and the rhinestones are just the easiest part to photograph.

## The internet might finally be tired of looking expensive

For a long stretch, the dominant online look was calm, neutral and very, very beige. Clean girl make-up. Quiet luxury. Minimal bedrooms with one plant and nothing on the floor. The goal was to look effortless, expensive and slightly unbothered.

Whimsymaxxing goes the other way on purpose. It is shiny, cluttered and a bit tacky, and that is the point. Speaking to Yahoo Lifestyle, one creative strategist put the swing down to aesthetic fatigue: after years of restraint, people want colour, personality and a little excess again.

There is a lot of history in the sparkle, too. The look borrows heavily from the rhinestone-everything era of the early 2000s, sometimes called McBling, when flip phones and velour tracksuits got the full gemstone treatment. Then again, recycling old aesthetics is what the internet does best. The same feed that brought back [the 2016 era of wired earbuds and flash photos](/culture/2016-is-the-new-2016-tiktok-revival) is now raiding an even older craft box.

## It arrives after years of trying to "lock in"

Look at what else has been trending. Routines, resets, productivity systems, five-step evening plans. Phases like [the September lock-in](/culture/what-is-the-september-lock-in) can be useful, but they also share an assumption: that your time should be improving you somehow.

Bedazzling a cup does not improve you. It does not build a habit, grow your following or prepare you for an exam. It is a small, slightly pointless project with a visible result, and that might be exactly why it feels good to people right now.

Craft has always had that quality. You sit down, you focus on something tiny, and for twenty minutes the only problem in your life is whether the pink gem goes next to the blue one. Nobody is grading it. Nothing is optimised. It is play, wearing an adult's clothes.

## A tiny rebellion against boring adulthood

There is also something quietly defiant about refusing to grow out of sparkly things. A lot of the aesthetic advice aimed at young adults amounts to "tone it down": neutral wardrobe, sensible decor, nothing that looks too young.

![A bright bedroom with pink botanical wallpaper, an orange bed piled with cushions and a wall covered in pinned photos](https://images.pexels.com/photos/6593740/pexels-photo-6593740.jpeg?auto=compress&cs=tinysrgb&w=1600)

Whimsymaxxing ignores that. It says your keys can have a charm on them, your laptop can have stickers, and your room can look like a person actually lives there. That links to other recent shifts toward imperfect, personal stuff, like [the return of digital cameras](/culture/why-everyone-is-using-digital-cameras-again) with their grainy flash and slightly wrong colours. In both cases, the appeal is something that looks like you made a choice, not like it came out of a template.

None of this requires a big budget. A sheet of stick-on gems is cheap. That accessibility is a big part of why the trend has travelled so fast.

## Of course, the internet can turn joy into shopping

Here is the catch. Anything that starts as "make your own fun with what you have" tends to get spotted by brands within about five minutes.

Fortune reported that companies from coffee chains to beauty retailers have been leaning into the look online, and that at least one smoothie shop had customers asking for their drinks in rhinestoned cups. The same piece compared the boom to the "lipstick index," the old idea that people buy small treats when bigger purchases feel out of reach.

That framing is worth sitting with. A tiny bit of sparkle can be a genuine bright spot. It can also become a reason to buy a pre-bedazzled version of something you already own, plus a new phone case, plus the matching charms. Whimsy that requires a shopping list is just regular consumerism with better lighting.

The version that keeps its charm is the low-effort one: decorating the thing you already have, using whatever is in the drawer, and accepting that some gems will fall off in your bag.

## Will whimsymaxxing disappear in three weeks?

Honestly, the word might. Internet trend names have a short shelf life, and "-maxxing" has already been attached to almost everything. Search interest in "whimsy" hit a peak in the summer, and the next aesthetic is always loading.

The impulse underneath it is harder to kill. People clearly want their stuff to feel personal, their days to include something that is just for fun, and their style to look like it belongs to a human rather than a mood board. That desire was around before rhinestones went viral, and it will be around after the sparkly cups are retired.

So if you have been eyeing that pack of gems, go ahead. Worst case, you end up with a very shiny phone case and a story about the autumn everyone bedazzled their coffee.
