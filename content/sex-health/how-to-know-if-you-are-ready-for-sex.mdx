There's no universal test, age, or relationship length that determines when someone is "ready" for sex — it's a personal decision that depends on your own feelings, values, and circumstances, not a milestone anyone else can decide for you. What follows is a framework for thinking it through honestly, not an answer about what you specifically should do.

## It has to be genuinely your own decision

Wanting to have sex because you actually want to is different from wanting to because of pressure — from a partner, from feeling behind friends, from thinking it's expected at a certain point in a relationship. If your main reason is anything other than your own genuine want, that's worth sitting with before anything else.

## Consent has to be clear, ongoing, and reversible

Consent means a clear yes, freely given, without pressure or manipulation — and it can be withdrawn at any point, even in the middle of something, for any reason or no stated reason at all. Anyone who reacts to a "no" or a "stop" with guilt-tripping, anger, or continued pressure is showing you something important about whether they actually respect your boundaries.

<Remember>
Being in a relationship, having said yes before, or having gone partway already does not mean consent is automatic going forward. Every time is its own decision.
</Remember>

## Emotional readiness matters as much as the physical decision

Feeling safe enough to talk openly with a partner — about what you want, what you don't, and any concerns — is a meaningful sign of readiness. If you'd feel too embarrassed or scared to bring up contraception, STI protection, or simply changing your mind partway through, that discomfort is worth paying attention to before deciding to move forward.

## Trust and communication with your partner

A partner who listens when you express hesitation, who doesn't pressure or guilt you, and who's willing to talk openly about protection and boundaries is showing you real signs of respect. A partner who dismisses concerns, rushes you, or makes you feel bad for wanting to slow down or wait is showing you the opposite, regardless of what they say about how they feel about you.

## Practical readiness: protection and health

Thinking ahead about contraception and STI protection — and actually having them available, not just planning to figure it out in the moment — is part of being genuinely ready, not just emotionally but practically. If that planning hasn't happened yet, that's a sign there's more to sort out before moving forward, not a detail to skip past.

## You're allowed to change your mind at any point

Even after saying yes, even mid-way through, even if you've done it before with the same person — you're allowed to stop, and a partner who respects you will stop immediately when you say so, no explanation required. This isn't a technicality; it's the actual, ongoing definition of consent.

## Questions worth sitting with honestly

- Is this genuinely what I want, separate from any pressure I'm feeling?
- Do I feel safe saying no or changing my mind, and do I believe that would be respected?
- Have we talked openly about protection and what happens if something goes wrong?
- Am I doing this out of curiosity and readiness, or out of fear of losing someone or falling behind?

## The bottom line

This isn't a decision anyone else — a partner, friends, the internet — can make for you, and there's no single right age or relationship stage that applies universally. If you can answer the questions above honestly and the answers point toward genuine readiness, mutual respect, and real communication, that's a far better indicator than any timeline. If they don't, that's worth listening to as well.
