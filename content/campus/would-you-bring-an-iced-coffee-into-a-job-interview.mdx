You are early.

Your outfit is fine.

You have checked the company website three times.

You have rehearsed “tell me about yourself” so many times that you are starting to sound like your own LinkedIn page.

Then you look down.

You are still holding your iced coffee.

Apparently, this is now a career question.

A recent TikTok from recruiter Caitlin Wehniainen kicked off a huge debate after she said some Gen Z candidates were turning up to interviews carrying iced coffee or iced lattes.

Her point was not that coffee makes someone bad at their job. It was about the impression.

To her, walking into a formal interview with a takeaway drink can make the meeting look like one more stop in your day instead of something you deliberately prepared for.

The internet, obviously, did not agree quietly.

Some people thought the advice was basic interview etiquette. Others had a simpler reaction:

Why are we judging whether someone can do a job based on what is in their hand?

And that is where the story gets more interesting than the coffee.

## The cup is not really the issue

Nobody thinks an iced latte changes your ability to write code, design a campaign, manage a project or answer a customer.

The argument is about signals.

An interviewer gets maybe 30 to 60 minutes with you. So they notice everything: how prepared you seem, how you speak to people, whether you understand the role and whether you look like you actually want to be there.

For some people, a takeaway drink gets mixed into that first impression. For others, it means absolutely nothing.

That difference is the entire problem.

## Professional according to who?

This is why the debate became bigger than one TikTok.

“Professional” sounds like a clear rule until you compare workplaces.

At one company, professional might mean a blazer, printed CV, no drink and formal language.

At another, the person interviewing you could be wearing sneakers, carrying their own coffee and asking to see your GitHub before asking about your degree.

Same word. Completely different culture.

That is what makes unwritten rules annoying. Nobody hands you a document called *Things This Particular Hiring Manager Will Quietly Judge You For*.

You usually find out afterward.

## Gen Z did not enter the same workplace

A lot of younger workers entered professional life through a very different setup: Zoom interviews, remote jobs, Slack, hybrid offices, casual dress codes and managers messaging instead of emailing.

For years, people interviewed from bedrooms and kitchen tables with a mug sitting next to the laptop. Nobody paused the interview and said, “Excuse me. Is that coffee?”

So it makes sense that some younger applicants do not automatically see carrying a drink as a serious etiquette problem.

The workplace became less formal, but some interview expectations did not move at the same speed.

## The interviewer can still care

You can think the rule is ridiculous. It can genuinely be ridiculous.

And the person deciding whether to hire you can still care about it.

That is the uncomfortable reality.

Hiring professionals are split on the issue. Some say coffee should not affect a hiring decision at all. Others think it can create an unnecessary distraction or look too casual in the wrong setting.

So there is no universal answer, which is exactly why this becomes a risk question.

If you bring the drink inside, maybe nobody notices. Maybe the interviewer has one too. Or maybe you have discovered the one person in the building with extremely strong feelings about iced lattes.

Is that fair? Not really. But interviews are full of tiny judgments that are not always directly connected to your ability to do the job.

## So should you bring it in?

If you want the lowest-risk answer: finish it outside.

Not because iced coffee is unprofessional. Not because you need to pretend to be a completely different person for 45 minutes.

Just because there is basically no upside to making your drink part of the interview.

You already have enough things to think about: your examples, your portfolio, the interviewer’s name, the questions you want to ask and that moment when they say, “What is your biggest weakness?”

Your iced latte does not need a subplot.

## The company matters more than TikTok

A law firm is not a gaming startup. A bank is not a design studio. A hospital is not a software scale-up.

Different workplaces have different levels of formality.

If you already know the company is casual, a coffee may not register at all. If the company looks traditional and you have no idea what the interviewer expects, leaving it outside removes one unnecessary variable.

That is not about surrendering to an ancient corporate rule. It is just reading the room.

## There are actual interview red flags

The debate becomes slightly funny when you compare coffee with things that genuinely matter:

- Showing up very late without saying anything.
- Not knowing what the company does.
- Lying about your experience.
- Interrupting constantly.
- Being rude to reception staff.
- Checking your phone every two minutes.
- Giving vague answers because you did not prepare any examples.

Those things can tell an employer something useful.

An iced coffee mostly tells them: you bought coffee.

That is probably why this debate annoyed so many people. The amount of meaning attached to the object feels much bigger than the object itself.

## Interviews work both ways

The company is forming an impression of you, but you are forming one too.

How do they speak to you? Did they respect your time? Did they explain the role properly? Does the office feel tense? Do the people seem normal? Could you imagine spending most of your week there?

If a company cares deeply about traditional etiquette, that tells you something about its culture. If another company barely notices your drink and wants to talk about what you built, that tells you something too.

Neither automatically means good or bad. But interviews are not only about convincing someone to hire you. You are also figuring out whether you want to say yes if they do.

## The safest move is boring

So you have an interview tomorrow. You are standing outside. Your iced coffee is still half full.

What now?

Honestly?

Finish it. Leave it outside. Throw it away if you have to.

Then go inside and give them something better to remember: your work, your ideas, your personality, your questions and your ridiculously over-rehearsed answer to “tell me about yourself.”

And if you get the job?

Bring the coffee on Monday.

Maybe get the large one.
