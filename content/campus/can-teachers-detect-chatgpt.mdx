You finish an assignment and read it one more time.

It sounds normal. Maybe even suspiciously normal.

Then somebody in the group chat says:

> “Bro, Turnitin can detect ChatGPT now.”

Suddenly one clean paragraph feels like it might trigger an academic investigation.

So, can teachers actually detect ChatGPT?

The short answer is: teachers can use tools that try to detect AI-written text, but those tools cannot reliably prove who wrote an assignment.

That difference matters.

## What can a teacher actually see?

Some schools and universities use plagiarism platforms or separate AI-detection tools. Turnitin’s [AI Writing Report guidance](https://guides.turnitin.com/hc/en-us/articles/22767652443357-AI-writing-detection) describes a report that can highlight qualifying text its model thinks may have been generated by a large language model.

But a report is not the same as a confession. Turnitin says its model can misidentify human-written and AI-generated text, and that the score should not be used as the sole basis for adverse action against a student.

It is an automated estimate. Not a tiny digital detective with a warrant.

## AI detection is not plagiarism detection

These are easy to mix up because they can appear inside the same academic platform.

A similarity report looks for text that matches material in other sources. AI detection tries to estimate whether writing has patterns associated with generated text.

That means an original paragraph can still look “AI-like” to a detector. It also means generated writing can sometimes avoid being flagged.

There is no perfect database containing every sentence ChatGPT has ever produced.

## Can a teacher ask ChatGPT if it wrote your essay?

They can ask. The answer still is not reliable evidence.

[OpenAI’s guidance](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8313351-can-i-ask-chatgpt-if-it-wrote-something) explains that ChatGPT cannot reliably identify whether it generated a particular piece of writing. If someone pastes an essay into a chatbot and asks, “Did you write this?”, the model can answer without actually knowing.

So a screenshot of ChatGPT saying “yes” is not proof.

## Why are AI detectors still used?

“Not proof” does not mean “completely useless.” A teacher may treat a detector result as one piece of information alongside the rest of the situation.

They may also notice that:

- your writing suddenly sounds very different
- your sources do not support what the assignment claims
- citations do not exist or cannot be found
- you cannot explain an argument you submitted
- your draft history does not match the final version
- your work breaks a clearly stated AI policy

That is why the useful question is not “Can I beat the detector?” It is “Can I explain how I produced this work?”

Trying to make writing look deliberately “less AI” can create a bigger problem than following the course rules.

## What if your university bans ChatGPT?

Then the detector question becomes almost irrelevant.

If your lecturer says not to use generative AI for an assignment, using it can violate the course rules whether a detector catches you or not. Different modules can also have different policies.

One lecturer may allow AI for brainstorming but not drafting. Another may allow it if you disclose how you used it. Another may prohibit it for a particular assessment.

Read the actual assignment brief. Not what somebody on TikTok says the rule probably is.

## What if AI is allowed?

Use it in a way you can defend. Depending on the policy, that might mean using AI to:

- brainstorm possible topics
- explain something you do not understand
- generate practice questions
- suggest a structure
- identify gaps in your draft
- plan revision

Your existing [guide to using AI for studying without cheating](/campus/how-to-use-ai-for-studying) covers that distinction in more detail.

The final responsibility is still yours. If an AI tool invents a journal article and you cite it, “the AI gave it to me” does not make the reference real.

## Keep evidence of your process

You do not need to turn every assignment into a criminal case file. For important work, keeping your normal process can help.

That might include rough notes, early drafts, version history, research links, feedback, calculations and your own outline.

This is useful even without AI. It shows how the work developed and makes it easier to explain your decisions if somebody asks.

## If your work gets flagged, do not panic

A flag is not automatically a finding of misconduct. Check the process your institution uses and ask what evidence is being considered.

Be ready to explain how you researched the assignment, what sources you used, whether you used AI, what the course policy allowed and what drafts or notes you have.

Turnitin’s guidance puts the final decision with the educator or institution, not the detector.

## The bottom line

Yes, teachers can use tools designed to detect AI-generated writing.

No, those tools cannot simply look at an essay and prove that ChatGPT wrote it. They produce estimates, and estimates can be wrong.

The thing that matters most is less dramatic: know your course rules, do your own thinking and be able to explain your work.

That is a better strategy than spending the entire assignment wondering whether your sentence sounds too grammatically stable to be human.
