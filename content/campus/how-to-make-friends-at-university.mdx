Almost everyone at university feels behind on making friends during the first few weeks, which is exactly why it's such a strange, misleading feeling — you're comparing your inside experience (uncertain, a little lonely) to everyone else's outside appearance (confident, already grouped up), when they're probably feeling the same thing you are.

## The friend groups you see are newer than they look

Most of the tight-knit groups you notice by week three formed in week one or two, often just from people who happened to sit near each other in orientation or ended up in the same dorm hallway. They look established because they've had a few weeks together, not because they had some head start you missed. New groups keep forming well into the semester — you're not too late.

## Say yes to the boring invites

Floor meetings, club fairs, "does anyone want to grab food" texts in the group chat — these feel low-reward compared to imagining some perfect friend-making moment, but they're where actual friendships start. Nobody meets their future best friend in a dramatic scene; it's almost always something forgettable like a shared study session that just kept happening.

<Callout>
Say yes more than feels natural for the first month. You can be more selective later once you actually know people — right now, you don't have enough information to know who's worth saying no to.
</Callout>

## Join something with a built-in reason to keep showing up

A club, an intramural team, a study group tied to a hard class — these work better than trying to make friends in the abstract because you have a repeated, low-pressure reason to be around the same people. Friendship tends to build through repetition more than through single great conversations.

## Your roommate isn't obligated to be your best friend

Plenty of people put pressure on a roommate relationship to also become a friendship, and it doesn't always work that way — sometimes a roommate is just a roommate, and that's fine. Don't let one disappointing roommate situation convince you that university friendships in general aren't working out; it's one relationship, not a verdict on your whole social life.

## Group chats are a real tool, not just noise

Dorm group chats, class group chats, club group chats — actually reading and occasionally responding in these (not just lurking) puts you on people's radar in a low-effort way. You don't need to be the funniest person in the chat. Just being a normal, responsive presence is enough to become a recognizable face when you do meet in person.

## It's okay if the first few tries don't stick

Not every club or group you try will be the right fit, and that's a normal part of figuring out where you belong rather than a sign you're doing something wrong. Trying a few different spaces in the first semester — even ones that don't turn into anything — narrows down where your actual people are more efficiently than sticking with the first option out of fear of starting over.

## Give it real time

Feeling settled socially at university usually takes a full semester, sometimes longer, not the first few weeks. If you're still figuring it out in October while it looks like everyone else has their group locked in, you're on a completely normal timeline — the appearance of everyone else being sorted is almost always more solid-looking from the outside than it actually is up close.
